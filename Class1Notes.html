01

What business analytics is

Business analytics is the scientific process of turning data into insights that help people make better decisions.

Three words in that sentence do most of the work.

Scientific means there are rules. You can't just look at some numbers, get a feeling, and put it in a chart. There are correct and incorrect ways to work with data, and the course will teach them.

Data means a record of what actually happened — not what someone remembers happening, and not what they think should have happened. People's memories are unreliable. Sales records are not.

Decisions is the whole point. If an analysis doesn't change what anyone does, it didn't accomplish anything, no matter how good the math was.

Core idea

Data has value only when it changes what we understand or what we decide to do. A large dataset by itself is not an insight, and an insight that cannot affect a decision may have little practical value.

The process works like a chain. It starts with data, which is usually messy, usually huge, and not very useful on its own. Then you apply tools and methods — this is what the course teaches. What comes out is information. And if the work was done well, you get an actionable insight: something specific enough that a manager can do something different because of it.

Companies that get this right tend to see five things: faster and better decisions, new products and services, cheaper and more efficient operations, higher profits, and happier customers. That last one isn't fluff. A customer who got what they wanted is a customer who comes back.

Consider a retailer deciding how much inventory to place in each store. A manager relying only on intuition may overlook differences in local demand, seasonality, promotions, and substitution across products. An analytics-based approach can combine those signals. The final decision still requires business judgment, because the model may not know about an upcoming competitor, a local event, or a supply disruption that is absent from the historical data.

Does any of this actually work? Researchers have checked. Brynjolfsson, Hitt and Kim looked at a large group of U.S. companies and split them into two groups: those that really used data to make decisions, and those that didn't. After accounting for how much each company spent on technology and other differences between them, the data-driven group had output and productivity about 5–6% higher. Those companies also did better on asset use, return on equity, and market value.

What 5% means

Five percent doesn't sound like much. But it's five percent of everything the company produces, every year, against competitors who are already trying hard. That gap is why companies keep spending money on analytics — and why they hire people who know how to do it.

02

Where the data comes from

Analytics works today because people generate an enormous amount of data just by living their normal lives. Scan a loyalty card at the grocery store, and that's a record. Search something on Google. Open Instagram. Click an ad — or don't click it, which also gets recorded. Even carrying a phone around creates a steady stream of location data.

Each of these is a small, ordinary thing. Added up, they form a trail that follows almost every customer through almost every day.

So data is now part of every business, not a side project at a few of them. Business analytics is the art and science of turning that data into decisions. Art, because deciding which question to ask takes judgment. Science, because once the question is set, there are right and wrong ways to answer it.

03

An example: doctors and machines

A study from 2016 shows the relationship between computers and human experts better than most. A team led by Andrew Beck, then at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and Harvard Medical School, worked with colleagues at MIT and entered a competition called Camelyon16.

The task was real medical work, not a puzzle invented for the contest. Doctors take a tissue sample from a lymph node, put it on a slide, and look for signs that breast cancer has spread. The team scanned those slides into digital images and tested how well a computer could read them.

Three results came out:

92%Machine alone
96%Doctor alone
99.5%Both together

Look at the first two numbers. The doctor beat the machine, 96% to 92%. When mistakes mean missed cancer, that is not a small gap. If you stopped reading here, the obvious conclusion would be that humans are better and the computer isn't worth using.

Now look at the third number. Let the computer scan the slide and mark the suspicious spots, then let the doctor make the final call, and accuracy goes to 99.5%. Errors drop from about 40 mistakes in every thousand cases down to about 5. Applied across the whole country, the researchers estimated that would mean roughly 56,000 fewer misread breast scans every year in the United States.

There are two lessons here.

The first: the right comparison is usually "human plus machine," not "human versus machine." The computer and the doctor made different kinds of mistakes. The computer caught things the doctor's eye skipped; the doctor caught things the computer misread. Each one covered for the other's weak spots. That is the real finding, not the raw score of either one.

The second: none of it works without data. Before the computer could do anything, the team had to gather a huge number of slide images and label each one correctly. Without that, there is no model. Not a worse model — no model at all. This is why the early weeks of this course are about handling data rather than about algorithms.

Source note

Beck et al., Camelyon16 challenge, ISBI 2016. Harvard Medical School wrote it up as “Better Together.” The original paper reports these results using a measure called AUC rather than plain accuracy; the numbers above follow the simpler version used in the lecture.

04

Who uses analytics

There is no part of business left where analytics is optional. It shows up in operations, logistics and supply chain, information systems, marketing, finance, accounting, sports, and health care. Here are real examples from several of those areas.

  • Shipping. UPS built a system called ORION that decides what order a driver should make their stops in. The company says it saves hundreds of millions of dollars a year, mostly by cutting the number of miles driven.
  • Entertainment. Netflix decides what shows up on your home screen. The company has said that most of what people watch comes from those recommendations, not from searching.
  • Sports. The Oakland Athletics story told in Moneyball is the classic case. A team with very little money used statistics to pick undervalued players and competed against teams spending several times more. Every major league team now has analysts on staff.
  • Theme parks. Disney's MagicBand system tracks where guests go and what they buy inside the parks. That data helps Disney manage lines, schedule staff, and stock food and merchandise almost in real time.
  • Retail. Amazon decides what to stock in which warehouse, what to recommend to you, and what price to charge. All three are analytics problems.

None of these are tech companies selling analytics. They are a shipping company, a streaming service, a baseball team, a theme park, and a store. In every case, analytics is something the business does inside itself, run by people who understand that business. That point comes back in section eight.

05

The four pillars

Analytics covers everything from a simple report to advanced optimization. The usual way to organize all of it is by the question being asked. Older textbooks list three categories: descriptive, predictive, and prescriptive. This course uses four, splitting out the "why did it happen" question as its own category, because in practice explaining a result is a different job from reporting it.

Descriptive

What happened?

Using data to understand what happened in the past, or what is happening right now. A dashboard is the most common form. Most companies start here, and that makes sense — you can't fix a problem you can't see.

What was our revenue in Texas in March 2020? Which factory had the lowest productivity last quarter? How many complaints did we get, how many did we fix, and what were they about?

Diagnostic

Why did it happen?

One step further. Descriptive tells you Texas sales dropped. Diagnostic figures out why — a price change, a competitor's sale, running out of stock, or unusual weather. This is where you dig into causes.

Why did our on-time delivery rate fall in the Southeast? Which products are dragging down our profit margin?

Predictive

What will happen?

You look at past data, find patterns in it, and extend those patterns into the future. Past sales become a forecast of future sales. Important limit: a prediction tells you what is likely to happen. It does not tell you what to do about it.

How will customers respond to this ad? How risky is this investment? How many units will we sell in November?

Prescriptive

What should we do?

This goes past predicting and recommends the best action, usually by testing all the options and scoring each one. Optimization and simulation live here. In practice you build this on top of a prediction, not instead of one.

How much should we produce next month? What's the cheapest way to ship from our factories to our warehouses? Which customers should get the discount?

Two things about this picture are worth remembering.

First, the pillars get harder from left to right, but not more important. A company with a great forecasting model and terrible reporting is in worse shape than a company with the opposite problem.

Second — and this is the main point of the diagram — all four pillars sit on data management: collecting, cleaning, organizing, combining, and reshaping raw data. What you can build on top depends entirely on how solid that foundation is.

06

What AI is

Ask most people to define AI and they'll name a product — usually ChatGPT. That answer isn't wrong. ChatGPT is AI. But it's like answering "what is a vehicle?" by naming one specific car. True, and misleading if you stop there.

Instead of picking one definition, compare three from organizations that have good reason to be careful about their words. Notice that they don't quite match.

NASA

Artificial intelligence refers to computer systems that can perform complex tasks normally done by human reasoning, decision making, creating, and so on. There is no single, simple definition of artificial intelligence, because AI tools are capable of a wide range of tasks and outputs. AI tools at NASA sometimes use machine learning, which uses data and algorithms to train computers to make classifications, generate predictions, or uncover similarities and trends across large datasets.

Google

Artificial intelligence is a set of technologies that enable computers to perform a variety of advanced functions, including the ability to see, understand and translate spoken and written language, analyze data, make recommendations, and more.

IBM

Artificial intelligence is technology that enables computers and machines to simulate human learning, comprehension, problem solving, decision making, creativity and autonomy.

As NASA suggests, there is no single, simple definition. AI is a catch-all term for many different techniques that make machines act more like people. The list of things under that umbrella keeps growing.

The useful takeaway: when a company says "our product uses AI," that sentence tells you almost nothing. Ask what the system actually does.

Why the definitions differ

Each definition highlights a different boundary of the field. Instead of asking whether a tool is "really AI," it is often more useful to ask what capability it provides, what data it uses, how it produces an output, and how much autonomy it has.

Two kinds of AI

For this course, one split matters most.

  • Traditional AI analyzes data, finds patterns, and makes predictions or decisions. The slide-reading system in section three is an example. So are fraud detection, demand forecasting, and credit scoring.
  • Generative AI creates new content — text, images, code, music. ChatGPT and Claude are examples.

Both matter and both come up in this course. But when a company talks about using AI to run its operations better, they usually mean the first kind.

AI, machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI

These terms are related but should not be used interchangeably. Each one sits inside the one before it.

Artificial intelligence

The broad umbrella. It includes different approaches for making machines perform tasks that appear intelligent or support intelligent behavior.

Machine learning

A subset of AI in which a model learns patterns from data rather than relying only on rules written explicitly by a programmer.

Deep learning

A subset of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers. It is especially useful for complex data such as images, audio, and language.

Generative AI

Uses models, commonly deep-learning foundation models, to create new content such as text, images, audio, video, or code in response to an input.

07

How AI and analytics fit together

People often treat AI and analytics as either rivals or as the same thing with different names. Neither is right.

Analytics turns data into descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive insight, using statistics, computing, and optimization. That's the building from section five. AI adds a floor on top. It can find more complicated patterns, make harder predictions, and handle unstructured data as well as structured data.

Structured data fits in rows and columns — a sales table, a customer list. Unstructured data doesn't: photos, emails, recorded calls, contracts, video. Until recently that material was nearly impossible to analyze at any real scale.

Notice where AI sits: above the pillars, not below them. It is a more powerful set of tools for predicting and deciding. It is not a replacement for clean, organized, trustworthy data. If a salesperson tells you their AI product means you can skip the data work, they are wrong.

08

AI and jobs

AI is changing the job market rather than simply wiping jobs out. But the change is not spread evenly, and three patterns are worth stating separately.

  • In some industries, the total number of jobs in AI-exposed work is going down.
  • That drop is concentrated in entry-level jobs — the repetitive, data-handling work that new graduates used to do, and that software can now handle well enough.
  • At the same time, middle-management jobs are growing, because companies need people who can read what these systems produce, decide what to do with it, and work between the technical team and the business side.

There is solid evidence for the second point. Hosseini Maasoum and Lichtinger studied U.S. résumés and job postings from 2015 to 2025. At companies that adopted generative AI, junior employment dropped about 9% within a year and a half, and the drop was largest in the jobs most exposed to the technology. Senior employment at the same companies barely moved.

One detail changes how you should read that. The drop came from slower hiring, not from layoffs. Companies weren't firing junior employees. They just stopped replacing the ones who left.

What employers are looking for

The pattern points toward jobs that need judgment rather than jobs that just need execution. Employers increasingly want people who have:

  • Real knowledge of the business. Understanding the situation the model is being used in, not just the model itself.
  • A sense of what makes a decision good. Being able to tell a good decision from a bad one, separate from whether it happened to work out.
  • An eye for where AI goes wrong. Knowing when a system is confidently wrong, when its training data was biased, and when it is missing context that matters.

This combination is sometimes called the AI manager. These people are not necessarily programmers. They understand what AI can and cannot do well enough to decide when it should be used, and they are responsible for making sure it gets used sensibly. The workforce ahead isn't just technology-driven. It's judgment-driven, with people directing AI instead of competing against it.

09

Why AI projects fail

Given how much money and excitement goes into AI, you might expect a record of steady success. It isn't. Estimates differ depending on how you count, but studies commonly find that 70% to 80% of AI projects fail to deliver the business value that was expected. Some findings are harsher. MIT Media Lab's Project NANDA, in an early 2025 report, found that only about 5% of custom enterprise AI tools ever made it out of testing and into real use, and that around 95% of the companies surveyed saw no measurable return at all.

Read those numbers carefully. They measure whether projects turned into working systems that paid off. They do not mean the algorithms didn't work. That distinction is the whole point of this section. These projects usually fail for management reasons, not technical ones. Six causes come up again and again.

  1. 01

    The wrong mix of people

    Companies hire plenty of data scientists and engineers but forget the AI managers — the project leads and business experts who connect the technical work to what the company is actually trying to do. Without them, teams build things that are impressive and useless.

  2. 02

    Bad data

    Garbage in, garbage out. On projects that succeed, something like 60–80% of the total effort goes into cleaning, combining, and checking data. Teams that rush this step find their models work in testing and fall apart in the real world.

  3. 03

    The company isn't ready

    Using AI is a workflow problem, not just a technical one. Many companies have no standard way to move data around, no version control for their models, and no habit of technical and business teams working together. Without that groundwork, even a good solution never scales past a pilot.

  4. 04

    Picking the wrong problem

    Teams often choose the flashy project that looks good in a presentation, then expect it to pay for itself in three to six months. The better question is boring: can AI actually add measurable value here? Projects succeed when they target a real problem the company already has.

  5. 05

    No oversight until it's too late

    Checks for fairness, bias, and legal compliance often get pushed to the end, when fixing anything is most expensive. Waiting creates legal risk, damage to the company's reputation, and unfair outcomes for real people. These checks need to start early, not after something goes wrong.

  6. 06

    Trusting the vendor too much

    Companies believe what a vendor promises without understanding how the model works or what it will really cost to connect it to their systems. Every company's data and workflow is different, and no off-the-shelf product fits perfectly. You need enough in-house knowledge to judge what you're buying.

Look at what's missing from that list. Not one of the six is a math problem. They are problems of planning, data discipline, organization, oversight, and leadership — the kind of thing a well-trained business student is better placed to fix than a specialist in model design.

Reference

Key terms

Business analytics
The scientific process of turning data into insights that help people make better decisions.
Data management
Collecting, cleaning, organizing, combining, and reshaping raw data so it can be analyzed. The foundation the four pillars stand on.
Descriptive analytics
Using data to understand what happened in the past or what is happening now. Reports and dashboards.
Diagnostic analytics
Figuring out why something happened — digging into causes rather than just reporting results.
Predictive analytics
Finding patterns in past data and extending them into the future to forecast what will happen. Gives a forecast, not a recommendation.
Prescriptive analytics
Working out the best action to take, usually through optimization or simulation. Normally built on top of a prediction.
Actionable insight
A finding specific enough that someone can do something different because of it. The goal of the whole process.
Artificial intelligence
A catch-all term for techniques that let machines do tasks normally needing human thinking, judgment, or creativity. There is no single agreed definition.
Machine learning
A part of AI that uses data and algorithms to train computers to sort things into categories, make predictions, or find patterns in large datasets.
Traditional AI
AI that analyzes data, finds patterns, and makes predictions or decisions. Fraud detection, demand forecasting, image recognition.
Generative AI
AI that creates new content — text, images, code, music.
Structured data
Data that fits into rows and columns, like a sales table or a customer list. What Excel is built for.
Unstructured data
Data with no rows-and-columns form — photos, emails, audio, video. Hard to analyze at scale until recently, and a big part of what modern AI opened up.
AI manager
Someone who understands what AI can and cannot do well enough to decide when and how it should be used, and to keep that use lined up with the company's goals. Not necessarily a programmer.
Decision support tool
A carefully built application made for a specific business situation and designed to be handed off so non-experts can use it. What you'll build in this course.
